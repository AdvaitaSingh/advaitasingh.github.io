Dealers of Direct Democracy: Strategic Initiation, Popularity and Agenda-Setting in Top-Down Referendums

BAFFI Master Theses Working Paper Series · 2025

This paper develops a theoretical model of the strategic incentives of incumbent politicians to initiate referendums. It formalizes the effect of referendums on general elections to identify two competing incentives for initiation: removing disadvantageous issues through unbundling and generating popularity from referendum wins. The model shows that referendum voting behavior determines which channel dominates. Sincere policy voting makes referendum outcomes predictable, strengthening incentives for popularity-motivated referendums, while probabilistic voting favors agenda-setting strategies. Electoral incentives can diverge from voter welfare. Strategic referendum initiation can generate welfare inefficiencies through the initiation of welfare-reducing referendums and the failure to initiate welfare-improving ones.

Can Money Tilt the Balance of Justice?

with Elliott Ash, Filippo Lancieri and Luigi Zingales

Conference on Empirical Legal Studies, 2026 · Northwestern University

This paper studies whether organized business interests influence judicial decision-making in state supreme courts. In 1998, the U.S. Chamber of Commerce created the Institute for Legal Reform and entered judicial politics by backing pro-business judicial candidates. Using this entry in a difference-in-differences design, the paper tests whether rulings shifted toward businesses on the issues the Chamber prioritized. To identify treated cases, it uses the Chamber's amicus briefs as a measure of its legal priorities and trains a text-embedding classifier to find similar cases in a corpus of state supreme court decisions. Preliminary results suggest that, after the Chamber's entry, businesses win more often in Chamber-relevant cases than in other cases, especially in states that elect their judges.

A Pro-Business Outlier? Benchmarking SCOTUS Against the Fifty State Supreme Courts

with Filippo Lancieri and Luigi Zingales

This paper examines the U.S. Supreme Court's pro-business shift since the 1970s, using the 50 state supreme courts as a benchmark. To do so, it introduces a new dataset of state supreme court cases from 1950 to 2023. The U.S. Supreme Court has risen from being the least pro-business court in the country during the Warren Court era to a pro-business outlier under the Roberts Court. Over the same period, aggregate business win rates in state supreme courts have remained stable at roughly 45–55%. Partisanship explains only part of these patterns, and its effect depends heavily on institutional design. After 2000, elected Republican-majority state courts became significantly more pro-business, while elected Democratic-majority courts became increasingly anti-business.
